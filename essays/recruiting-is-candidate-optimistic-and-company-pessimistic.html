Recruiting is Candidate Optimistic and Company Pessimistic

October 8, 2026

If you've taken CS 180 at UCLA, I'm sure you're familiar with the Gale-Shapley Algorithm. If not, it is essentially an algorithm to generate a "stable matching" between two disjoint sets.

From a recruiting perspective, if we match Company A with Candidate A and Company B with Candidate B, this is a stable matching if and only if there is no "exchange" both parties would be happy with. For example, if we proposed a match between Company A and Candidate B instead, at least one of the two parties must be unhappy about this outcome in order for this matching to be stable.

The Gale-Shapley algorithm comes very close to how recruiting actually works, at least in tech. Say a company stack ranks candidates. When this company extends offers to candidates, they extend enough offers to fill their headcount. Then, as certain candidates reject the offer (because they have received an offer from another company which they rank higher), the company proceeds to offer candidates further down in the stack ranking. Once the company has finally filled their headcount, then they stop extending offers.

Notice that this behavior very closely mirrors the Gale-Shapley algorithm. Keep in mind this is not a perfect representation of recruiting, as not all companies stack rank, nor do they necessarily have extremely rigid headcounts. There are a plethora of reasons as to why Gale-Shapley is not a perfect reflection of how real-life tech recruiting works, but for our purposes it works well enough to draw some parallels.

For those more familiar with the Gale-Shapley Algorithm, you may be aware of the optimality of the stable matching it provides. There is a fun proof which shows why Gale-Shapley is offerer pessimistic and offeree optimistic, which I would highly encourage exploring if you have the time. However, it is enough to know that it produces the worst possible stable matching for the offerers (companies), and the best possible stable matching for the offerees (candidates).

I see many candidates who are very pessimistic when it comes to the state of the tech job market. I am not attempting to invalidate those experiences, or claim that the job market isn't actually getting worse. I just hope to provide some perspective and hopefully inspire a little bit of optimism within people who are actively searching right now. I believe it is worthwhile to think critically about how the job market functions, and why things are the way they are rather than simply accepting it.

Here are some tangentially related questions which I believe are also worth thinking about:

  • If the job market truly is oversaturated with talent, why are big tech companies still willing to pay such high salaries?
  • How much money do you truly need to be "happy"? How much to be "secure"? How much to be "comfortable"?
  • For those who want to pursue quant, what is the actual marginal utility of the money which quant provides compared to say, big tech?
    • Is the additional effort it would take you to get that "better" or higher paying job worth the marginal utility of that money?
  • What is the gap between you and the version of you (or someone else) who is capable of landing your "dream job"?
  • Is it truly just more "good" companies on your resume?
    • If so, how do you fix this cyclical logic (needing good previous role to land good future role)?
    • If not, how do you most effectively close that gap?
