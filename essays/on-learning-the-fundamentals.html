On Learning the Fundamentals

October 9, 2026

Why even learn the fundamentals? Is there any point if AI is capable of doing the basics way faster than you could? I mean, for plenty of people, AI is capable of doing everything not only way faster, but also better than most people, so what even is the point of learning anything?

These questions sound rational, up until you substitute AI with literally any other technological innovation that makes our work more efficient. Why know any basic facts at all when you can simply search for the answer on the internet? Why learn how to perform addition when you can simply use a calculator and do the "higher level" thinking yourself?

These questions sound stupid, but why are they fundamentally different from the ones being asked about AI? At their core, we have some "higher level" thinking which requires learning and understanding the "lower level" fundamentals. For example, it would be crazy to imply that someone is capable of understanding algebra without even having a basic grasp on arithmetic.

Sure, within the computer science space we may have many useful abstractions which allow us to ignore these "lower level" concepts (These abstractions also exist in math, mathematicians don't always understand the first principle proofs of theorems that they use). However, in order to perform some meaningful "higher level" thinking, you typically need to understand at least the "lower level" concepts directly tied to what you're attempting to dive deeper into.

Believing that you do not need to understand the fundamentals implies one of two things. Either, you are delegating all of the "lower level" fundamental thinking to AI without a proper understanding of what is going on, and are thus incapable of performing tasks with depth greater than AI (and at that point, do you even provide any useful input?). Or, you believe that somehow, without any understanding of the fundamentals, you are capable of performing "higher level" thinking (i.e. understanding algebra without even a basic grasp of arithmetic).

In the first case, you will never understand the fundamentals because you constantly delegate your thinking and understanding of these topics to AI. Therefore, you will not be improving and never be able to perform "higher level" thinking and problem-solving because you never learn the fundamentals. Then, how would you differentiate yourself from literally anybody else with the same AI tool in their hands?

If you genuinely believe the second case, and you think my analogy is completely inaccurate, then that's fair. I would just ask why you believe it is possible to think about an interesting problem with sufficient depth to solve it without even understanding the fundamentals, and if you could provide a concrete example of such a situation.

If you don't want to be in the situation of the first case, and you don't genuinely believe in the second case, there is no (as far as I'm aware) rational reason to not learn the fundamentals. Of course, this does not mean that you have to completely stop using AI, especially if you're actively employed and require it to ship fast enough for your job.

When considering how to use AI, or how to even approach whatever you're doing right now (work, school, personal projects, etc.), I would strongly recommend thinking about what objective function you are optimizing for.

For example, at school, I am purely focused on optimizing for my learning, so I tend to avoid AI use as much as possible (and not optimize for grades, but rather learning). At my internship at Tesla, I was primarily focused on actually being productive and shipping fast, so I would use AI whenever I believed it would make my work more efficient (which was very often).

Note that your objective function does not have to be literally only focused on one thing. For example, at Tesla, although my main priority was productivity, that doesn't mean that I didn't put time and effort into making sure I was genuinely learning and improving over time.

I would also recommend properly defining what you are optimizing for. For example, attempting to optimize for your career is not equivalent to optimizing for your internship next summer. Instead, you should genuinely take some time to reflect upon what career you are attempting to optimize for. Is it the job you land right after graduation? Is it the overall trajectory of your career? Is it your internship next summer? Do you want prestige, pay, work-life balance, or something else?

Realistically, there will be no singular correct answer, but instead a combination of various factors. Ultimately, I think it is worth seriously thinking about your objective function, properly defining your objective, and then taking time to think about what is the best approach to optimize for said objective function. If your objective function is drastically different from mine, it may genuinely be better for you to never learn the fundamentals and simply use AI. However, that is your decision to make, not mine.
