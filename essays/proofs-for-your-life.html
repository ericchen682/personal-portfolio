Proofs For Your Life

October 10, 2026

If you've ever taken discrete math, I'm sure you're familiar with the concept of proofs. One key thing to note is that proofs cannot exist in a vacuum. They are either reliant on other theorems which have been proved in the past, or on axioms which have generally been agreed upon by the wider mathematical community.

I believe that you can apply the same logic to your own life.

Unlike mathematics, your personal axioms are something only you can define. It is worth taking some time to think about what truly is an axiom of your life. My personal axioms are focused upon maximizing my overall happiness and fulfillment throughout my life.

In my personal opinion, I think axioms should primarily be centered around emotions, and the difference in your axioms compared to another person's should be the importance upon which you place each emotion.

For example, someone with a strong focus on instantaneous happiness may live a very hedonistic lifestyle. You may judge them, and think that you would never want to live a life like theirs. However, they are living life properly according to their axioms (of course not all people who lead hedonistic lifestyles actually hold these axioms).

Once you have defined your personal axioms, I encourage you to think about your values, and consider whether or not they truly accurately represent your axioms. Of course, this will not (and should not) be as formal of a process as a mathematical proof. However, it is worth thinking about, even if the "proofs" will be a little hand-wavy.

Once you have a set of axioms and "proven" values, it becomes much easier to rationalize your way of thinking. Then, in important circumstances, you can continuously ask yourself "Why?" and boil your thought process and emotions down to axioms. By doing so, you are capable of either proving your rationale (and having more conviction), or disproving it (and adjusting your behavior accordingly).

One personal example is how I've handled circumstances in which there is pressure to perform (public speaking, tests, interviews, etc.). A lot of people (including me in the past) feel nervous in these circumstances.

Why did I feel nervous in these circumstances?
Oftentimes, it was because I felt some kind of pressure to perform.

Why did I feel pressure to perform?
Because I cared about the outcome, and I was scared I would mess up.

Now, there are two questions to be asked.
Why did I care about the outcome? Why was I scared I would mess up?

Oftentimes, I didn't actually care about the outcome all that much. In those circumstances, I was being irrational (and thus shouldn't have been nervous in the first place). This realization helped me not be nervous when I didn't care about the outcome, because if I didn't care about the outcome that much, why would I be so afraid of messing up?

Now, let's handle the case where I actually do care about the outcome.
If I cared so much about the outcome, and was scared of messing up, what is the correct thing to do in this circumstance?
For me personally, it was to spend more time preparing, so that I would feel so ready that I wouldn't be afraid of messing up.

What if I didn't care enough to prepare to that extent?
Then, I reach a contradiction with my original assumption that I care about the outcome. It doesn't make sense for me to care about the outcome, yet be too lazy to spend time preparing and practicing. Therefore, the initial assumption (that I care about the outcome) must be false.

In all cases, I end up reaching the conclusion that it is irrational to be nervous.
